Who leads, and why — by axis, not by company

Mobility belongs to the acrobats and the veteran: Boston Dynamics (self-righting, battery self-swap, two decades of dynamic locomotion), Unitree (3.3 m/s record, flips at consumer prices), EngineAI (the world's first humanoid front-flip and the most humanlike straight-knee gait), and Robot Era (4 m/s claimed on the L7). Note how little this axis predicts commercial rank — the mobility leaders and the deployment leaders barely overlap. Manipulation is where the West still leads: Figure's 16-DoF tactile hands doing four-minute autonomous dishwasher cycles, 1X's 25-DoF force-controlled tendon hands, Tesla's 22-DoF hand hardware, and — the stranded asset of the sector — Sanctuary's 20-DoF hydraulic haptic hands, now for sale without a robot attached. China's manipulation story is thinner: strong grippers and arm dynamics (Astribot's 10 m/s end-effector), weaker fine-motor autonomy. Autonomy is the axis where the scoring is most punitive, because teleoperation dressed as autonomy is the sector's endemic disease. The credible high scores are Figure (Helix, with the strongest — though still largely company-published — evidence of long unattended runs), Galbot (independently visited unmanned stores), Toyota's TRI (frontier LBM research, verified on Atlas), and AgiBot's GO series. The explicit penalties: 1X (teleoperators are the business model), Sanctuary (teleop-first methodology), Tesla (repeated post-hoc teleop reveals), and most of the Chinese demo circuit (CNN found rental humanoids ship with a human operator at ¥3,500/day).

Maturity is Chinese: Unitree's 5,215 audited 2025 units and AgiBot's ~5,100 (together over half of global volume, on IDC's ~18,000-unit 2025 estimate) tower over every Western maker — though both fleets skew research and education, not labor. The most auditable Western maturity is Agility's: 65,000+ operating hours and nine customer facilities, now stated in SEC filings. Cost is a rout: Unitree and Noetix set a price floor ($1,400–$16k) that no Western OEM approaches; 1X's $20k NEO is the only Western entry within an order of magnitude. Traction splits by kind: UBTech has the deepest verified factory roster (BYD, Foxconn, Zeekr, FAW-VW), Agility the most verified hours, Figure the marquee BMW logistics deployment (40 units, per BMW itself) — while Tesla, XPeng, and the entire R&D tier have effectively none.

Design approaches — what the architecture choice commits you to

Every row's design approach is a bet about which constraint binds first, and the choice largely pre-determines the commercial path. Industrial bipeds (Tesla, Apptronik, Kepler, UBTech — typically linear roller-screw or heavy servo-rotary actuation) buy stiffness, payload, and positional repeatability at the cost of mass and inherent danger: an 70–100 kg rigid robot cannot safely share space with untrained people, so this architecture is structurally confined to factories and warehouses — which is fine, because that's where the near-term money is. Compliant/tendon-driven design (1X is the pure case: ~30 kg, backdrivable, tendon transmission) inverts the trade — NEO is engineered to be incapable of hurting you, which is the entry ticket to homes, but the same compliance caps payload and precision, and softness makes control harder, which is partly why 1X leans on teleoperators. Dynamic bipeds (Unitree, EngineAI, Robot Era — torque-dense quasi-direct-drive rotary, RL locomotion) optimize for the thing that goes viral: speed, flips, fall recovery. It's cheap to build and spectacular to watch, but athleticism generates almost no labor revenue — which is why the mobility leaders and the deployment leaders in this table barely overlap.

The sharpest split is at the end-effector. Dexterity-first designs (Figure, Sanctuary, XPeng, Holiday) bet that general-purpose hands are the moat: if a robot can manipulate anything a human can, its addressable market is all manual labor. The cost is brutal — hands are the most failure-prone, most expensive, hardest-to-control component on any humanoid, and they multiply the AI burden. Task-specialists and gripper pragmatists (Agility above all, Astribot, Galbot) make the opposite bet: pick a task, design the cheapest reliable end-effector for it, and start billing. It is no accident that the only Western humanoid with SEC-auditable operating hours (Agility) is the one that refused to build hands, and the most-verified autonomous deployment in China (Galbot's stores) runs on grippers. Wheeled manipulators (Galbot, Rainbow, Astribot, LG, Humanoid, GAC's transformer) apply the same pragmatism to locomotion: wheels give up stairs and rough terrain in exchange for uptime, stability, battery life, and roughly half the bill of materials — and the verified commercial deployments to date skew heavily wheeled. Legs are a bet that brownfield human spaces (stairs, ladders, vehicle interiors) matter enough to pay their premium; wheels are a bet that most paid work happens on flat floors. Service anthropomorphs (Fourier, Chery, Xiaomi) sell human acceptance rather than labor — soft shells, faces, dialogue — a smaller but nearer market. And the AI/software-first rows (Toyota's LBM work, TARS) decline the hardware fight entirely, betting the durable margin sits in the model layer, as it did in autonomous driving.

The investment implication: architecture is destiny on timing. Gripper-and-wheels designs monetize now but cap their ceiling; dexterity-first bipeds carry the largest theoretical prize and the longest, most capital-hungry road; dynamic bipeds win attention and research budgets while they search for a job. Watch for convergence plays — AgiBot and Apptronik hedge by shipping both wheeled and biped variants, and Leju's modular biped↔wheeled conversion is the hedge built into a single chassis.

The production ledger — maturity, year by year

If maturity ultimately picks the winners, the question is not "who shipped the most" but whose numbers can you believe, and whose ramp is real. Evidence quality in this sector comes in four grades: audited (prospectus/annual-report/SEC-grade — only Unitree, UBTech, and Agility qualify), third-party verified (Omdia counts, press-toured factories, customer press releases), company-claimed, and speculative (targets contradicted by track record or with no track record at all). Three patterns dominate the table below. First, the only audited multi-year ramp in the industry is Unitree's 5 → 410 → 5,500+, and the only four-digit audited full-size figure is UBTech's 1,079 — everything else at volume is claimed or analyst-counted. Second, nameplate capacity runs 30–100x ahead of output almost everywhere: Figure's line is ~3% utilized against its 12,000/yr nameplate, Agility's RoboFab ~1%, and Leju's brand-new 10,000/yr line has published no output since opening. The winners will be whoever closes that gap — not whoever announces the biggest number. Third, the industry denominator roughly matches: ~2,300 global units in 2024, 13,000–18,000 in 2025 (Omdia vs IDC), and a 2026 forecast cluster at ~50,000 (Morgan Stanley China, Deutsche Bank global, TrendForce) — meaning 2026 company targets, added up, exceed the most bullish analyst forecast by a wide margin. Someone's guidance is wrong.

Mispriced cells

Sort by CAP, then by MKT, and the divergences fall out. High CAP, weak MKT — the M&A shelf: XPeng's IRON (CAP 6.3 against MKT 2.7) is the sector's biggest gap, though it is captive inside a listed parent; the actionable versions are Robot Era (fastest legs, real VLA, thin order book), Astribot (world-class arm dynamics, no blue-chip customer), LimX (credible locomotion, modest traction), and Sanctuary (the best hands in the industry inside a distressed cap table — a components acquisition waiting to happen). The precedents are set: Mobileye paid $900M — 5.5x the last private mark — for pre-revenue Mentee, and Kelin's Kepler takeover shows what the distress exit looks like. High MKT, weak CAP — the story-risk shelf: Chery's Mornine (a 2-hour battery selling on consumer e-commerce) and Noetix (toy-price volume) monetize novelty, not labor. Hidden robot content in public tickers: Hyundai 005380.KS is priced as an automaker while owning the sector's best locomotion; Samsung 005930.KS holds Rainbow, a humanoid-specific solid-state battery program (SDI, H2 2027), and the foundry contract for Tesla's Optimus chips — three separate humanoid exposures inside one chaebol discount.

Method & disclaimer

Formulas. CAP = (MOB + MAN + AI) / 3 · MKT = (MAT + COST + TRAC) / 3 · Overall = CAP×0.40 + MKT×0.60 — equal factor weights within each axis, maturity-tilted overall. Scores 0–10 are analytical judgments applied to the raw metrics shown under each chip, not measurements. Teleop-dependent demos are penalized heavily on the AI factor. "(claim)" marks company-sourced numbers; "Not disclosed" marks unpublished data — the two are deliberately distinguishable.

Flagged uncertainties. (1) The 2025 volume crown is contested: Unitree's prospectus claims 32.4% share vs Omdia/Xinhua crediting AgiBot ~39% — methodology differs; both shipped ~5,100–5,500. (2) Nearly every speed, payload, and runtime figure in this table is company-claimed; no independent benchmark standard exists. (3) Nameplate capacity ≠ output: UBTech (6,000/yr), Leju (10,000/yr), Tesla (1M/yr) are all claims against far smaller audited deliveries. (4) Figure's 2024 BMW scope was disputed by Fortune (one robot, non-production hours) before BMW's own June 2026 confirmation of 40 units — treat all founder-sourced fleet numbers cautiously. (5) Apptronik's DoF specs conflict across sources (28–71); Dobot's likewise (28 vs 41). (6) Unitree's ticker does not exist yet; Agility's AGLT has not begun trading; AgiBot's HK IPO is unfiled-to-pending. (7) Hand-metric anchors held consistent with the companion hands table: Tesla 22-DoF, Figure 03 16-DoF/3g tactile, 1X 22+3-DoF, Unitree Dex5-1 16-active. (8) Sector context: ~330 Chinese humanoid models were unveiled in 2025; K-Scale, Aldebaran, and Embodied already shut down — consolidation is underway.
