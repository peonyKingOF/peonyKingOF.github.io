Section 2 — Analysis

Is Ukraine winning the drone war despite being out-produced?

"Based on the media I thought Ukraine was basically winning in drone combat — with more casualties inflicted. Am I wrong, and how does this jive with Russia greatly outstripping Ukraine in production?"

The two facts are less contradictory than they look. Ukraine really is winning the casualty exchange. Per CSIS, the Russia–Ukraine casualty ratio ran between 2:1 and 3:1 for much of the war but has likely risen to nearly 8:1 in the first half of 2026, largely due to Ukraine's drones — including AI-enabled ones. Ukrainian officials claim drones caused 96% of Russia's ~34,000 casualties in March, and that Russia suffered 316 casualties per square kilometer captured in early 2026, up from 120 the year before. (Many of these are Ukrainian government figures, though independent analysts broadly agree on direction and rough scale.)

The production totals count different things

A large share of Russia's output is Shahed/Geran long-range drones and cheap Gerbera decoys aimed at Ukrainian cities and power grids — terror and infrastructure weapons, not frontline killers. They barely register in the military casualty ratio. Strip those out and the frontline picture is much closer; a Ukrainian presidential official claimed Ukraine has actually achieved a 1.3:1 advantage in FPV drones at the front, whatever the aggregate national numbers say.

Effectiveness per drone matters more than raw count

Independent analysis holds that Ukraine's data integration and AI-assisted targeting extract more effect per drone deployed than Russian equivalents. Ukraine pioneered the confirmed-strike incentive system, fiber-optic FPVs, and AI terminal guidance that defeats jamming. Volume is Russia's answer to a quality gap.

The tactical situation is asymmetric

Russia is the attacker. Its infantry must cross open ground in view of Ukrainian drones, while defenders stay dispersed and dug in. Drones punish movement above all — Russian military bloggers have estimated frontline life expectancy in some sectors at 20–35 minutes. If the roles flipped, Russian drones would impose the same tax on Ukrainian attackers.

Defense also counts

Ukrainian interceptor drones downed a reported 33,000 Russian UAVs in March alone — a meaningful slice of Russia's production advantage erased in the air before it does anything.

Synthesis: Ukraine is winning the drone effectiveness war decisively; Russia is winning the drone production war and betting it can eventually convert mass into results — through Shahed saturation of cities and by closing the innovation gap. The casualty ratio shows Ukraine's approach working today; the production trend is why analysts still describe the overall picture as a strategic stalemate with Ukraine holding tactical innovation leadership, rather than a Ukrainian victory in progress.
Section 3 — Analysis

Why hasn't AI completely supplanted the human FPV pilot?

"How is it that FPV has not been completely supplanted by AI by now?"

Mostly economics and the messiness of real battlefields — not because the AI is impossible.

Cost per unit is the tyranny of the whole system

The FPV model works because the drones are $500–$2,000 and expendable — both sides burn through hundreds of thousands per month. Full onboard autonomy means a real compute module, better (often thermal) cameras, and more power, which can double or triple unit cost. At these volumes, that's the difference between fielding 100,000 drones a month and 40,000. When your doctrine is saturation, a cheaper dumb drone plus a trained human often beats a smarter, scarcer one. The AI chips are also exactly the components hardest to source at millions-of-units scale.

AI has taken over — but only the last kilometer

What's widespread is terminal guidance: the pilot flies the approach and designates the target; the AI locks on and completes the strike even if jamming severs the link. Systems like Ukraine's Hornet go further — onboard AI that identifies targets and distinguishes decoys by geometry and thermal signature without a satellite link. But that's autonomy for the final phase, not the mission. The hard parts — searching a treeline, deciding a tarp-covered lump is a howitzer and not a decoy, prioritizing among targets, aborting because friendlies moved, threading a flight through a dugout entrance — are still done better and more adaptably by humans. Retraining a pilot takes a conversation; retraining a vision model takes data collection, validation, and a fleet-wide firmware push.

Fiber optics relieved the pressure

The strongest argument for full autonomy was electronic warfare — if jamming cuts the radio link, the drone must think for itself. The fiber-optic FPV solved jamming a different way: an unjammable physical cable with a human brain on the other end. Both sides got a cheap path to keeping humans in the loop precisely when autonomy advocates expected EW to force them out.

Trust and waste

An AI drone that misclassifies burns the airframe, reveals the launch position, and possibly hits the wrong thing. Pilots provide real-time verification, battle damage assessment, and mid-flight re-tasking that current autonomy handles poorly. And the human infrastructure is enormous: Ukraine flies on the order of 11,000 drone sorties a day, with schools, unit tactics, and trained operators that carry their own momentum.

Trajectory: More autonomy is clearly coming — AI interceptors, swarm coordination — but as a gradient (assistance → terminal lock-on → autonomous search in defined kill boxes) rather than a switch flip. The human FPV pilot persists for the same reason infantry does: cheap, adaptable general intelligence is still hard to beat at $2,000 a round.

The numbers make a few things obvious. The market has already found this theme: the small-cap drone names (ONDS, UMAC, RCAT) and MP trade at 15–29x sales while mostly losing money — the Pentagon procurement story is priced up front. The cheapest exposure is where drones are buried inside something bigger: Sony at 1.5x, Lantronix at 2.1x, Honeywell at 2.6x, AeroVironment at 3.9x, Qualcomm at 4.7x. Teledyne at 5.0x is arguably the best balance of direct exposure and sane valuation.

Structural caveats. The most interesting AI-drone companies — Anduril, Skydio, Shield AI, Neros — are private, so public exposure to the pure thesis is mostly indirect through suppliers. For the mega-caps, drones are a sliver of revenue: you're buying the whole company, not the drone story. EV/Sales comparisons across profitable mega-caps and pre-profit micro-caps say more about risk appetite than relative value.
